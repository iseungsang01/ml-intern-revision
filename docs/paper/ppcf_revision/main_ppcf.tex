\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Charge-exchange spectroscopy (CES) measures the ion temperature at the pedestal top of KSTAR, where the measurement is weakest: values are missing, scatter from frame to frame and include failed fits. Monitoring and control need a value for every 10\,ms frame that is formed without waiting for the next one, a nowcast. We look for the causal estimator of the CES ion temperature on the pedestal-top chord with the lowest error against holding the last value, a causal Gaussian process and interpolation that waits for the next value, and ask what fast diagnostics at the same location, beam-emission spectroscopy and electron-cyclotron-emission imaging, add. With every value up to the previous frame at hand, the estimator with the lowest error that we found needs no neural network: a Gaussian process on the past CES values followed by a boosted regression on scalars of that history and of the fast channels, about two milliseconds per frame on one processor thread. Both components are standard; the contribution is the measured comparison for this chord. On 627 discharges of the 2022 campaign and under four validation designs, its mean squared error (MSE) on the value of the frame is lower than that of monotone cubic interpolation, which waits for the next value, by \TXoverPchipVfoura{} per cent in the least favourable design (chronological; 95\,\% interval, clusters of shots resampled), and lower than that of a two-sided Gaussian process by \TXoverOfflineVtwo{} per cent when clusters of shots are held out; in time order no difference from the latter is established. An estimate from the CES history alone does not reach the two-sided process: the fast diagnostics carry the difference in the random and cluster-held-out comparisons. In use, where the CES value is late or sparse, the fast diagnostics matter more: with the newest CES value \AgeMedianS\,s old they reduce the MSE of the estimate of the temperature level by \IncDsixfiftyVthree{} per cent (rolling origin), and on a second device, MAST, with Thomson scattering, by \IncMastDhundredMthree{} per cent at 100\,ms. The advantage over interpolation lies in the larger errors; the median error of the two-sided process is slightly lower. A separately audited temporal convolutional network, fitted under a fixed budget with and without the fast channels, is reported as a comparison and is not adopted: with the previous frame visible its fast-enabled form is not established better than its history-only form under any design and is worse in time order, and its stale-history increment depends on the validation design, availability condition and evidence scope. The second stage has to be refitted for the condition of use, the gain is not shown to be specific to the radius, and under the beam-modulation schedule of the present analysis it is not established for the last block of shots of the campaign. No independent campaign and no temperature reference other than the reported CES value were available.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} charge-exchange spectroscopy, ion temperature, pedestal, nowcasting, real-time estimation, beam emission spectroscopy, electron cyclotron emission imaging, KSTAR, MAST

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection*{a. Objectives and research plan}
This paper evaluates a causal \emph{nowcast} of the reported carbon-impurity CES temperature on one pedestal-top chord of the KSTAR 2022 campaign; the data provider selected the chord. At each frame of a 10\,ms grid the estimator receives two kinds of input: past CES reports made visible by the specified availability rule, and current and past BES and ECEI voltage snapshots. From these it estimates the CES report belonging to the current frame before that report is available.

The work proceeds in two stages. The first-stage plan compares history-only and lightweight models, including CNN and causal temporal-convolution candidates, with reference estimators. Fair comparisons require matched rows and information sets, with computation budgets stated explicitly. Completed comparisons retain their registered support and budget restrictions; they do not establish a budget-matched ranking of every architecture. The aim is to test whether other diagnostics add useful temporal information to the CES history. In the second, the measured errors, modality contributions and costs from that comparison are used to design a neural network for the task. This second stage is a plan; it is not reported here as a result.

Three comparisons organize the paper:
\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item Which causal estimators are useful when scored against three references? The references are hold, a causal Gaussian-process estimator, and two-sided interpolation of the monotone piecewise-cubic family~\citep{fritsch1980}. The last needs the next report, so it serves only as an offline reference (\S\ref{sec:res:best}).
\item What information do BES and ECEI add to the CES history (\S\ref{sec:res:fast})?
\item Do the answers persist under four validation designs, which are given equal weight, and does the computation fit within a 10\,ms frame (\S\ref{sec:res:designs})?
\end{enumerate}
Optional application analyses (\S\ref{sec:res:use}) and the conditions under which the estimator does not help (\S\ref{sec:res:limits}) follow.

Two targets are scored. F is the CES report of the current frame. L5 is a level computed offline as the median of measured values in the next five grid rows, requiring at least three measured values. Both are targets only: neither the current F nor the future-derived L5 is an online input.

\subsection*{b. Why the problem is worth studying}
The edge ion temperature of H-mode plasmas changes on several time scales. On KSTAR, CES has shown the $T_i$ pedestal building up over tens to hundreds of milliseconds after the L--H transition and falling during ELM suppression by resonant magnetic perturbations~\citep{Ko2015IonTemperature}. On ASDEX Upgrade, a dedicated fast system recorded edge impurity temperature collapsing and recovering within a few milliseconds of type-I ELM crashes~\citep{Viezzer2018IonHeat}.

Diagnostic requirement studies also motivate timely ion-temperature estimates. Specifications developed for FIRE call for edge $T_i$ profiles at 10\,ms and about 10\,\% precision~\citep{Young2008PlasmaMeasurements}, and the ITER specifications list edge $T_i$ at 100\,ms and 10\,\%~\citep{Donne2007ChapterDiagnostics}. These are design targets for other devices, not KSTAR requirements. Charge-exchange $T_i$ has also served directly in feedback: on JT-60U, neutral-beam power regulated a two-chord core $T_i$ difference within a 10\,ms control cycle~\citep{Yoshida2009RealTime}.

CES infers impurity temperature and rotation from the Doppler width and shift of a line excited by charge exchange with beam neutrals~\citep{Fonck1984DeterminationPlasma}. The KSTAR calibration study describes observation of a C\,VI charge-exchange line~\citep{Ko2010ChargeExchange}. In the beam-modulation analysis described for KSTAR, the background of a beam-on frame is interpolated from the beam-off frames that bracket it~\citep{Lee2020TwoGaussian,Lee2026NeuralNetworkbased}. The authors of the former argue that this analysis cannot begin before the following beam-off frame. That is an argument about the procedure, not a measurement. We did not record when our reports became available, and we assign them no delay.

The reports are also conventional labels rather than independent physical truth. Active and passive emission must be separated in the analysis~\citep{Viezzer2011PassiveEdge}. On DIII-D, main-ion and carbon temperatures have been measured to differ near the edge~\citep{Haskey2018MainIon}. Occasional fit failures have been associated with abrupt outliers in KSTAR CES profiles~\citep{Kim2024KineticProfile}. The simplest nowcast holds the last report, and this is the reference against which any added information must be shown.

\subsection*{c. Expected outcome}
The intended product is an estimate for frames whose CES report is not yet available, together with an assessment of its error. The assessment reports matched-row error differences against the references, the dependence on the age and availability of the CES history, the contribution of each modality, the sensitivity to validation design, and the computation time. Together these indicate when such an estimator could be used.

The estimator is complementary to spectrum reading. An upstream reader such as NN-CES converts a recorded spectrum into a report~\citep{Lee2026NeuralNetworkbased}. Our downstream estimator works from the report history and needs no current CES spectrum. A faster upstream reader would refresh the anchors on which the nowcast rests, and it could equally shrink the interval in which a nowcast is needed. Which effect prevails must be measured. We did not implement this coupling.

Meeting a computation budget is not a real-time deployment, and no physical mechanism is identified. The results concern one chord in one reused campaign, with no untouched data.

\subsection*{d. Relation to prior work}
Neural networks trained on conventionally fitted spectra have long been used to read charge-exchange line parameters. At JET, networks gave approximate line parameters~\citep{Bishop1993AutomaticAnalysis}, and a later monitoring demonstration produced $T_i$ profiles at the 50\,ms exposure cadence~\citep{Svensson1999AnalysisJet}. A recent example comes from HL-3~\citep{Tian2026RealTimeCxrs}. At DIII-D, a simplified real-time fit is reported to deliver values every 5\,ms with a delay of a few milliseconds; its agreement with the offline analysis is lower on edge chords than on core chords~\citep{Chrystal2026RealTime}. On KSTAR, a two-Gaussian fit~\citep{Lee2020TwoGaussian} and NN-CES~\citep{Lee2026NeuralNetworkbased} both dispense with beam-off frames. The NN-CES authors report close agreement with the modulation analysis inside $R\approx2.2$\,m and weaker agreement farther out. We have not examined NN-CES on our chord and claim no comparison with it.

Our task differs from forecasting future profiles. Neural-network forecasting has, for example, predicted profile changes about 200\,ms ahead from present profiles and proposed actuator trajectories~\citep{Abbate2021DatadrivenProfile}. A further line of work estimates missing or slow measurements from other signals. A DIII-D real-time kinetic reconstruction, trained with input dropout, still returns profiles, including an ion temperature profile, when charge-exchange data are absent; that ion temperature was assessed qualitatively~\citep{Shousha2023MachineLearningbased}. Faster diagnostics have been mapped to Thomson-scattering electron profiles at DIII-D~\citep{Jalalvand2025MultimodalSuperresolution} and COMPASS~\citep{Imrek2026TemporalSuperresolution}. In an offline replay of TCV data, a Kalman observer combines fast interferometry with slower Thomson scattering~\citep{Pastore2023ModelBased}. We do not suggest that this literature is noncausal or restricted to electron quantities.

The present question is timed differently. It concerns one ion-temperature chord, with past reports of the same diagnostic as anchors. Its fast inputs are BES~\citep{Lampert2015CombinedHydrogen} and ECEI~\citep{Yun2014Quasi3d} voltage snapshots. Edge ECEI can respond to density as well as temperature~\citep{Vanovac2018EffectsDensity}, so the snapshots are treated as uncalibrated predictors, not as calibrated $T_e$ or $n_e$ and not as full-rate turbulence measurements. The estimators are built from familiar models, and no algorithmic priority is claimed. The contribution is an application-specific empirical evaluation.


\section{Theoretical background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Pedestal and ion-temperature measurement}
The pressure at the top of the H-mode edge barrier is widely regarded as a strong determinant of global confinement, and pedestal-top values serve as boundary conditions for core transport models~\citep{Snyder2011FirstPrinciples}. On KSTAR the $T_i$ pedestal spans a few centimetres. It is usually characterized by fitted heights and widths~\citep{Ko2015IonTemperature}. This study uses one chord that the provider designated as pedestal-top. It does not redetermine the pedestal position for each discharge.

\subsection{From a CES spectrum to a reported temperature}
Charge exchange between neutral-beam atoms and fully stripped carbon produces excited ions emitting the C\,VI line. The active emission is weighted near the beam--line-of-sight intersection; passive emission along the line of sight can overlap it~\citep{Fonck1984DeterminationPlasma,Isler1994OverviewCharge,Viezzer2011PassiveEdge}.

For a simplified Gaussian Doppler line, let $W_D$ be its standard deviation in wavelength after accounting for instrumental broadening, and $\lambda_0$ the rest wavelength. The carbon-ion temperature in energy units satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \Theta_C \equiv k_B T_C = m_C c^2\left(\frac{W_D}{\lambda_0}\right)^2 .
\end{equation}
Here $m_C$ is the carbon-ion mass and $c$ is the speed of light. The relation omits effects such as unresolved fine structure; actual spectral analysis must account for the instrumental response and line shape~\citep{Fonck1984DeterminationPlasma,Lee2026NeuralNetworkbased}. In the delivered files the energy-unit carbon-impurity temperature is denoted $T_i$. We use these reports and do not fit raw spectra.

The result is therefore a carbon-impurity temperature produced by a defined analysis chain. It is not necessarily the main-ion temperature: the two have been measured to differ in the edge of DIII-D~\citep{Haskey2018MainIon}. NN-CES learns the same fitted parameters from raw spectra~\citep{Lee2026NeuralNetworkbased}. It reads spectra faster, but its reference remains the conventional analysis.

\subsection{Spectrum reading versus report availability}
Reading a spectrum and having a report available are separate steps. Once a spectrum has been read, its report can be appended to the history. Before that, the current report can be estimated only from information already present.

Let $\mathcal{I}_t$ denote the past CES reports made visible by the specified availability rule at frame $t$ together with the BES and ECEI snapshots up to and including $t$. Our causal estimator is restricted to $\mathcal{I}_t$, sharing the past-and-present information restriction of filtering. Its supervised target is a reported quantity, rather than an identified physical state. Two-sided interpolation also uses later reports and has a smoothing-like information set~\citep{Shukla2020SurveyPrinciples,VanMulders2025ModelBased}. It is therefore an offline reference.

The targets are F, the report of frame $t$, and L5, an offline median of measured values in the next five grid rows, requiring at least three measured values. Neither is ever part of $\mathcal{I}_t$. Nowcasting here means estimating the pending report of the current frame. It does not mean forecasting a future physical temperature, and a finer output grid alone does not demonstrate improved measurement resolution. In particular, intra-ELM dynamics below the delivered grid are not independently resolved by the present targets.

\subsection{Candidate learning models}
The candidates differ in how they use $\mathcal{I}_t$:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
\item Hold carries the last report forward.
\item A causal Gaussian process expresses smooth temporal correlation in the report history.
\item Recurrent and history models carry a state across frames. The elapsed time since the last report can be supplied explicitly as a feature~\citep{Che2018RecurrentNeural}.
\item Local temporal convolutions, including causal temporal convolutional networks, apply filters over a recent window of snapshots. They can detect short-lived patterns without a long memory.
\end{itemize}
Which of these helps is decided by matched comparisons of regression error and uncertainty, not by architecture alone. The completed comparisons and the planned network design are separated in Section~\ref{sec:intro} and in the Methods.


\section{Materials and methods}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data set: files, chord and collection criteria}
\label{sec:data:files}

We use the \NFiles{} discharge files of the KSTAR 2022 campaign (shots 30801--32751) on a 10\,ms grid; 639 discharges have more than one grid row (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). The plasma current, the field and the density are not in the files. Each row holds nine BES channels~\citep{Lampert2015CombinedHydrogen,KFE2026DiagnosticsDataUserGuide}, four ECEI channels~\citep{Yun2010DevelopmentKstar}, two Mirnov-coil signals (not used by the estimator of this paper), the time stamp and the CES values of one outboard chord at $R = 2.198$\,m, selected by the data provider as the pedestal top within the BES coverage. No equilibrium is in the files, so the normalized radius of the chord is not fixed for these discharges; the published positions, given in different coordinates and for different discharges, are collected in \Supp{sec:sdata}. We call it the pedestal-top chord and do not claim that it sits at the pedestal top in every discharge. A second set of files gives, for the same rows, the power and voltage of the six neutral-beam sources (\SuppTab{tab:nbisources}).

The first and the last row of a file carry no CES value and are not grid rows; the grid of all files has \NGridRows{} rows. In \StuckShare{} of them, in \NStuckDischarges{} discharges, the four ECEI channels are constant for at least ten rows while the BES channels are not; these rows are kept (\SuppTab{tab:stuck}).

\subsubsection{Campaign and device-state classification}
All files used here belong to the KSTAR campaign identified above. A shot-level classification by confinement regime, RMP state or device modification has not been established from verified metadata. The validation partitions below are statistical divisions, not physical discharge classes. A future campaign/device-state comparison requires verified operational metadata and a separate registered analysis.

\subsection{Absent frames and the age of a value}
\label{sec:data:late}

Inside a discharge the step between rows is 10\,ms, except for steps of 20 to 40\,ms: one to three frames are absent. In the beam files these steps are notches of the two sources of the first beam box that run: in the first row after such a step both are low together, while the sources of the second box are not (\SuppTab{tab:nbinotch}). The absent frames are the frames exposed without the beam that CES views, from which the analysis takes its background. The CES values in the files are the product of the analysis after the discharge. When each of them could have existed during the discharge depends on how the analysis is scheduled; if, following the description of the KSTAR analysis~\citep{Lee2020TwoGaussian}, the frames between two notches become analysable once the second notch has been taken, and the analysis itself takes no time, the newest value that could have been delivered is \AgeMedianS\,s old at the median, with quartiles \AgeQuartLoS{} and \AgeQuartHiS\,s (Fig.~\ref{fig:problem}b). We call this reading the \emph{beam-modulation schedule} and use it in \S\ref{sec:res:use} as one condition of use, not as a fact about an existing system.

\subsection{The reported value}
\label{sec:data:value}

The quantity is the temperature that the KSTAR CES analysis reports for the chord: the temperature of C$^{6+}$ ions from the Doppler width of the C\,VI charge-exchange line~\citep{Ko2010KstarCesSystem,Ko2010ChargeExchange,Ko2015IonTemperature}. Whether it equals the main-ion temperature is a property of the measurement that this paper does not test; in the pedestal the two can differ~\citep{CanoMegias2025MultiIon,Haskey2018MainIon}. The files carry no per-frame fit error and no spectrum. Values above 3\,keV, most frequent at low beam emission and taken as failed fits~\citep{Kim2024KineticProfile}, and values that repeat the previous one to the last digit, which are held values and not measurements, are treated as missing everywhere: as targets, as history inputs and as scored rows.

The reported value scatters from frame to frame. In eleven discharges with raw 500\,kHz BES, readings that rise for one frame are several times more frequent in frames whose exposure contains a burst of BES light, consistent with edge-localized modes, than their share of the frames; in those frames the level of the following frames and the rotation from the same fit do not move (a registered test, \SuppTab{tab:bursts}). Without spectra or fit errors it cannot be decided whether the ions are hotter in such a frame or the fit is disturbed. This is why a second target, the level around which the readings scatter, is carried beside the value of the frame.

\subsection{Predictor design and evaluation}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsubsection{Targets}
\label{sec:methods:target}

With $y_t$ the reported \Ti{} of row $t$, the nowcast target is $y_t$ itself, the \emph{value of the frame} (F in the tables): no estimator reads it. It is the only target on which interpolation can be compared, since interpolation fills row $t$ from the values on both sides of it. Because a single reading scatters (\S\ref{sec:data:value}), the \emph{level of the following frames} is carried beside it,
\begin{equation}
  L_t = \operatorname{median}\bigl\{\,y_{t+j} : 1 \le j \le 5,\ y_{t+j}\ \text{measured}\,\bigr\},
  \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
defined when at least three of the five are measured (L5 in the tables). The interpolation references read rows inside this window and are not scored on it. What the fast diagnostics add (\S\ref{sec:res:fast}) and the estimator in use (\S\ref{sec:res:use}) are scored on the level, which is less sensitive to one-frame excursions of the reading. Both targets are built from reported values: they are not an independent measurement of the bulk-ion temperature, a bias of the analysis that lasts longer than 50\,ms is in them as well, and L5 is a median over following frames, not an instantaneous temperature.

\subsubsection{Neural-network architecture and modification criteria}
Architecture selection is an empirical question. The intended first stage compares CNN or lightweight temporal candidates with history-only models and the reference estimators. Regression errors, uncertainty, modality contributions and computational costs determine whether and how a later CES-specific network should be modified. Completed comparisons below retain their stated training budgets and scoring support; they are not evidence that every CNN candidate has already been assessed. Subsequent redesign remains a research plan requiring registration before new fits.

\subsubsection{Estimators and references}
\label{sec:methods:est}

Table~\ref{tab:inputs} lists the estimators. \emph{Hold} keeps the newest visible value. The \emph{causal GP} is a Mat\'ern-3/2 process~\citep{rasmussen2006} on the last 16 visible values, with hyperparameters chosen by marginal likelihood at every row. The \emph{history-only estimator} T0 and \emph{the estimator of this paper}, TX, add to the GP estimate $g_t$ a boosted regression~\citep{Friedman2001GreedyFunction,Pedregosa2011ScikitLearn} $f$ that is fitted on the fit discharges to the target minus $g_t$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat y_t^{\mathrm{T0}} = g_t + f_0(\mathbf h_t), \qquad
  \hat y_t^{\mathrm{TX}} = g_t + f_X\bigl(\mathbf h_t,\, \mathbf q_t(b),\, \mathbf q_t(e),\,
  \mathbf a_t(b),\, \mathbf a_t(e)\bigr).
  \label{eq:est}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf h_t$ holds ten scalars of the visible history (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}), $b$ is the mean over the nine BES channels and $e$ the mean over the four ECEI channels, each channel standardized with its mean and standard deviation over the fit discharges. For either array mean $x$ the four scalars of the present are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf q_t(x) = \bigl(x_t,\; x_t - \bar x_t,\; s_t,\; x_t - x_{t-1}\bigr),
  \label{eq:fast}
\end{equation}
with $\bar x_t$ and $s_t$ the mean and the standard deviation of $x$ over the ten rows before $t$, and the three scalars that tie the present to the \emph{anchor row} $a(t)$, the row of the newest visible CES value, are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf a_t(x) = \bigl(\tilde x_{a(t)},\; \tilde x_t - \tilde x_{a(t)},\;
  \ln(\tilde r_t / \tilde r_{a(t)})\bigr),
  \label{eq:anchor}
\end{equation}
where a tilde is the mean over the ten rows that end at the index and $r$ is the array mean of the raw, unstandardized channels. The regression is used with its default settings, without tuning, one fit per target and condition, and is the same on KSTAR and MAST. TE and TB are TX with the ECEI half or the BES half of the fast scalars only; TN and TXN are T0 and TX with 19 scalars of the beam waveforms added. The GP and the boosted regression are standard methods; what is specific to this work is their arrangement for this chord and the scalars of equations~(\ref{eq:fast}) and~(\ref{eq:anchor}).

Two \emph{references wait for the next value} and are not available in real time: monotone cubic interpolation (PCHIP)~\citep{fritsch1980} through the measured values of the block with row $t$ left out, and an \emph{offline GP}, the same Mat\'ern-3/2 process as the causal GP with the 16 nearest values on each side of row $t$ instead of the 16 before it. For both processes the values are standardized within the neighbour set, and the length scale (5 to 80\,ms) and the noise are chosen at every row by marginal likelihood on a fixed grid. A recurrent network (a two-branch LSTM) and a two-stage form of it, the estimators of an earlier version of this work, are comparison rows.

A temporal convolutional network (TCN), an established architecture, was evaluated afterwards as a further comparison row in two forms: a \emph{history TCN} that reads the visible CES history only, and a \emph{fast TCN} that reads the fast channels as well. The architecture was selected on validation data among the candidates of a pilot, and every fit ran under a fixed wall-clock budget. In the audited result files and in the latency table of \S\ref{sec:res:tcn} these two networks carry the labels TH and TX. That TX is the fast TCN and is not the estimator of this paper, and TH is not T0. Everywhere else in this paper TX and T0 denote the GP-and-boosting estimators of equation~(\ref{eq:est}), and the networks are called fast TCN and history TCN.

We call an estimator \emph{causal} when it uses no sample stamped later than the row and no statistic computed over the discharge that is scored; statistics of the fit come from fit discharges only. Causality is by time stamp: when a sample would arrive at a control system is not established by the files. ``Fast'' refers to availability: BES and ECEI deliver a sample without an analysis delay. The files hold one sample per 10\,ms row, and nothing here uses the bandwidth of these diagnostics.

\subsubsection{What the estimator may see}
\label{sec:methods:rules}

The \emph{nowcast condition} is that every CES value up to the previous frame is visible (D10 in the tables); the fast samples and the beam waveforms are visible up to $t$. For the estimator in use (\S\ref{sec:res:use}) three further \emph{availability rules} restrict the CES values (Fig.~\ref{fig:rules}): D$d$, values stamped at $t - d$ or earlier, for $d$ of 50 to 1000\,ms, with D650 the median age under the beam-modulation schedule; S, values up to the last frame before the most recent notch, that schedule with an analysis time of zero; and K$k$, values of every $k$-th frame only, a CES that reports more rarely. A delay $d$ is the age of the visible CES history, not a forecast horizon: under every rule the target stays at row $t$ (F) or in the frames just after it (L5).

For an estimate at row $t$ (dashed line) the fast samples are visible up to $t$; the CES values that are visible are those up to the previous frame (D10, the nowcast condition) or, for the estimator in use, those older than a delay $d$ (D$d$), up to the last frame before the most recent notch (S), or of every $k$-th frame (K$k$). \subsubsection{Two controls}
\label{sec:methods:controls}

Statements about the fast inputs are accompanied by two controls. In the first, the fast scalars are permuted over rows and the estimator is refitted: the reduction against the history-only estimator should then be zero. In the second, the fast scalars are taken at the anchor row instead of row $t$: it asks whether the samples of the present are needed, or whether fast samples as old as the CES value serve as well.

\subsubsection{Validation designs, scores and intervals}
\label{sec:methods:designs}

All KSTAR numbers of the tables generated with the main analysis come from one definition of the data: 639 discharges, the 10\,ms grid without the first and last rows of the files, values above 3\,keV and held values missing everywhere, segments cut at gaps of 0.5\,s or more that no estimator crosses, and all estimators of a table scored on the same rows (\SuppTab{tab:sets}).

Four validation designs are used, and the tables show all four; no row is called primary.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \textbf{V1, random}: five folds of whole discharges, three re-partitions.
\item \textbf{V2, clusters held out}: five folds of whole \emph{shot clusters} (runs of shot numbers with steps of at most three), three assignments.
\item \textbf{V3, rolling origin}: the shot list in fifths; fifths two to five are scored with an estimator fitted on the fifths before.
\item \textbf{V4, chronological}: fitted on the shots up to 31991 and scored on the shots from 32312 on; the fit up to 32310 is shown beside it. The second fit shares one shot cluster with the scored block (shots 32310 and 32312); the first shares none.
\end{itemize}

Errors are in eV. The tables give the root-mean-square error (RMSE), the mean absolute error (MAE), the median absolute error and the share of rows whose estimate lies within 10 per cent of the target, and, for an estimator A against a reference R on the same rows, the \emph{reduction of the MSE} in per cent,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta(A, R) = 100\,\bigl(1 - \mathrm{MSE}(A)/\mathrm{MSE}(R)\bigr).
  \label{eq:delta}
\end{equation}
The \emph{fast-channel increment} is $\Delta(\mathrm{TX}, \mathrm{T0})$ under one condition. For a rule $\rho$ of \S\ref{sec:methods:rules}, the \emph{cost of staleness} and the \emph{recovered share}, the part of the loss that TX gives back, are
\begin{equation}
  C_\rho = \frac{\mathrm{MSE}_\rho(\mathrm{T0})}{\mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{D10}}(\mathrm{T0})},
  \qquad
  \phi_\rho = \frac{\mathrm{MSE}_\rho(\mathrm{T0}) - \mathrm{MSE}_\rho(\mathrm{TX})}
  {\mathrm{MSE}_\rho(\mathrm{T0}) - \mathrm{MSE}_{\mathrm{D10}}(\mathrm{T0})},
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
all on the same rows. Intervals are 95\,\% bootstrap intervals from 20{,}000 draws in which shot clusters are resampled (under V1 and V2 the re-partition or assignment is drawn first); intervals with discharges resampled are narrower and are in the result files. A difference is called established only when the cluster interval excludes zero. All intervals condition on the fitted estimators: they carry no uncertainty from refitting, from selection or from the number of statements. Strata are defined on inputs or on the time structure only, never on the scored value. The example discharge of the figures was chosen by a rule and not by eye: among the test discharges of one re-partition with at least 500 scored rows under rule S and at least three notches, the one with the median fast-channel increment.

\subsubsection{Second device}
\label{sec:methods:mast}

On MAST we use the public FAIR-MAST data~\citep{Jackson2024FairMast}: the charge-exchange \Ti{} of chords in a pedestal-top band, 1.5 to 5\,cm inside the edge of the Thomson density profile, in 5\,ms frames, with the Thomson-scattering \Te{} and density at the same radius as inputs of the present (nine scalars: present and anchor-frame values, their difference and log ratio, and the distance to the density edge). Thomson scattering on MAST is not faster than the charge-exchange frames: what is tested there is a present measurement of another diagnostic against a stale charge-exchange history. The target is the level of the next five frames. No frame is hidden by a beam modulation on MAST, so delays D5 (previous frame) to D150 are imposed; the designs are M1 random, M2 sessions held out (a session is an experiment date), M3 rolling origin and M4 chronological, with sessions resampled for the intervals. T0 and TX are built as on KSTAR.

\subsubsection{Registration and independent recomputation}
\label{sec:methods:reg}

Each batch of analyses of the main line of this paper (labelled B.73 to B.86 in the supplementary material and the repository) was registered before it was run: its statements, the rule by which each is judged, the expected outcome and the sentences that the paper would carry for either outcome were committed first. Choices that a registration left open were written down before the readout. A treatment changed after a readout had been seen is shown as a sensitivity beside the registered readout, not in its place. For every batch the inputs and one fit, or the statement values, were recomputed with code written independently of the implementation. The statements that do not hold, with the expectations that were not met, are in \Supp{sec:sstatements}. Diagnostics that were not registered are marked as read after the fact, and where they carry no interval this is said.

The TCN comparison of \S\ref{sec:res:tcn} is a later batch. Its queue ran to completion, an audit after the run passed, and the selection and the readout were checked to rest on identical records. It reuses the campaign and the fit and validation data on which earlier choices were made. Its tables are generated from the audited result file and are not edited by hand. Any proposal that follows from its test readout is exploratory.

On the value of the frame the RMSE falls along the ladder: hold, PCHIP, the causal GP, then the history-only estimator and the offline GP close together, and the estimator of the paper lowest (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}). The nowcast, which does not wait, has a lower MSE than PCHIP, which does, under all four designs; the least favourable is the chronological design (Table~\ref{tab:vsref}). Against the offline GP, the stronger of the two references that wait, the MSE is lower under the random and the cluster design, and in the two designs that respect time order the interval contains zero: a difference from an interpolation that has seen the next value is not established there, in either direction. The history-only estimator has a higher MSE than the offline GP under all four designs; where the nowcast passes the offline GP, it is the fast diagnostics that carry it there.

The advantage lies in the larger errors. The median absolute error of the offline GP is slightly lower than that of the nowcast (against PCHIP the difference of the medians is not established), while the 90th percentile and the mean squared error of the nowcast are lower (lower block of Table~\ref{tab:vsref}): an interpolation with the next value in hand is hard to improve on in a quiet frame, and the nowcast gains where the value moves.

On the level of the following frames the estimator of the paper has the lowest error of the causal estimators of Table~\ref{tab:ladder}, and it is closer to the level than the reading of the frame itself: with the previous frame visible, two thirds of the estimates lie within 10 per cent of the level, against three fifths of the readings (Table~\ref{tab:robust}). The two-stage form of the recurrent network has the same error as the estimator of the paper on the value of the frame, the network alone a higher one, and both have a higher error on the level (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}): for the nowcast condition this network is not needed. One recurrent architecture was compared, and the regression is untuned. The budgeted TCN of \S\ref{sec:res:tcn} was not scored on the rows of Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and is not ranked in it.

\subsection{What the fast diagnostics add}
\label{sec:res:fast}

\emph{Question: what do BES and ECEI at the location add to the CES history?} With the previous frame visible the interval of the fast-channel increment on the value of the frame excludes zero under all four designs (under the chronological design only just; the spread over the seeds of the regression, measured for an older history in \SuppTab{tab:seeds}, was not measured for this condition), and on the level under three; under rolling origin its interval on the level contains zero (rows ``TX over T0'' of Table~\ref{tab:vsref}; the two halves and further estimators in \SuppTab{tab:dten}). It is small: the CES value of the previous frame already says most of what can be said about this one. It grows when the CES value is older (\S\ref{sec:res:use}), and the following properties were measured there, with the newest CES value \AgeMedianS\,s old.
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \emph{Controls.} With the fast scalars permuted over rows the reduction is zero within its interval in eight of ten cells, and the estimator with the present fast samples has a lower MSE than the one given the fast samples of the anchor row under every design (\SuppTab{tab:controls}): the increment rests on the samples of the present and their change since the newest CES value, not on a memory of the state of the discharge.
\item \emph{Which diagnostic.} Neither half carries the increment under all four designs by itself: the ECEI half alone fails under rolling origin, the BES half alone under the chronological design; the two together hold under all four (\SuppTab{tab:which}).
\item \emph{Beam waveforms.} The beam power, known without any diagnostic, does not explain it: with 19 beam scalars given to both estimators the fast diagnostics still reduce the MSE under all four designs (\SuppTab{tab:which}).
\item \emph{Beam notches.} Under the random design the increment is not confined to the rows after a notch, where the reported \Ti{} and the BES light dip together: it is established on rows more than 300\,ms after one and on rows with no notch before them. With the history \AgeMedianS\,s old it is not established far from a notch under the two designs in time order; under the schedule and rolling origin it is (\SuppTab{tab:tau}).
\item \emph{Specificity.} Fast inputs of another discharge at the same time in the discharge give no reduction. The two Mirnov signals, which are not at the radius, treated exactly like the fast inputs, carry about a quarter of the increment under the random design, and under rolling origin the estimator with the fast inputs is not established above the one with the Mirnov inputs (\SuppTab{tab:specificity}). By the rule registered beforehand we therefore do not claim that the increment is specific to the radius of the chord. No channel-specific pedestal mechanism is established.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Other divisions of the discharges, time order and real time}
\label{sec:res:designs}

\emph{Question: does it depend on how the discharges are divided into fit and test, and is it fast enough?} Neighbouring shots resemble each other, and a campaign drifts; a random division answers neither. In Table~\ref{tab:vsref} the point estimates fall from the random design to the designs that hold out clusters or respect time order, and the intervals widen: part of what a random division shows is the resemblance of neighbouring shots. What holds under all four designs with the previous frame visible is the lower MSE against hold, the causal GP and PCHIP, and the fast-channel increment on the value of the frame. The intervals do not include the variability of the fit; over ten seeds of the regression a chronological increment moves by a few hundredths (\SuppTab{tab:seeds}).

Formed one row at a time on one processor thread, the GP and the regression together take about two milliseconds at the median and less than five at the 99th percentile (\SuppTab{tab:timing}): the estimate fits into a 10\,ms frame with the resources at hand. This is the time of the estimator only; fast samples delivered in real time are published for BES at DIII-D~\citep{Kim2026RealTimePlasma} and not for KSTAR.

\subsection{In use: a second device}
\label{sec:res:mast}

\emph{Question: is the direction peculiar to KSTAR and to ECEI and BES?} On MAST the
electron channel is Thomson scattering, an instrument with another light source and
another detector. With the charge-exchange history 100\,ms old the Thomson scalars reduce
the MSE of the level estimate under all four designs; the least favourable is the
rolling-origin design (MAST rows of Table~\ref{tab:increment}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}b,
\SuppTab{tab:mast}). Read after the fact on the chronological fit, without an interval,
the \Te{} scalars alone give nearly the whole increment and the distance to the density
edge gives none (\Supp{sec:schecks}). A closed form that multiplies the held \Ti{} by the
ratio of the two \Te{} values is not usable: the ratio of two Thomson values scatters too
much; a regression is needed. The pedestal-top band is defined with the Thomson density
profile that also supplies the inputs, the scored part of the chronological design is
small (39 sessions), and both halves of the MAST data had been read in earlier batches.

\emph{From the core to the edge.} The KSTAR files hold one chord and the channels around
it. On MAST the charge-exchange chords and Thomson scattering cover the whole radius, and
the same estimator was refitted in five bands from the pedestal top to the magnetic axis
(\SuppTab{tab:mastradius}). With the history 100\,ms old the Thomson scalars reduce the MSE
of the level estimate in every band under the random and the rolling-origin design; with
the previous frame visible they do so in every band under the random design, and under
rolling origin in every band except the pedestal-top one. Against what was registered as
expected, a stale history costs more, and the Thomson scalars add more, inside than at the
pedestal top. The share of estimates within 10 per cent of the level is higher inside and
stays below seven tenths in every band. On the value of the frame the interpolations that
wait have the lower MSE in every band inside the pedestal-top band; in the pedestal-top
band the interval contains zero. These are statements about MAST: the KSTAR files hold no
channel inside the pedestal top.

\subsection{A budgeted temporal convolutional comparison}
\label{sec:res:tcn}
The selected temporal convolutional architecture was evaluated as a fast-input model and a matched history-only model under mixed CES availability. This is a budgeted comparison, rather than a replacement decision for the GP-and-regression estimator. The full validation suite has one initialization; the fixed subset has three. These evidence scopes are kept separate in the following generated tables. They must not be pooled as evidence of initialization stability over the entire suite.

\input{generated_tables}

The paired skill measures the reduction in squared error from adding the fast inputs to the matched history model. Its sign and interval depend on both validation design and CES availability. The random design provides evidence of a positive increment under several stale-history conditions. The clustered and chronological designs do not establish that increment generally, and some fresh-history comparisons give negative intervals. An interval containing zero does not establish equivalence. These results therefore do not support adopting the selected neural estimator across all designs and conditions. Availability delay is the permitted age of CES history, not a prediction horizon.

Both models were trained under fixed computational limits, with checkpoint selection using validation data. Consequently, the results compare the reached fits under those limits; they do not establish an architectural ordering at convergence. Intervals condition on fitted models and omit training, checkpoint-selection and multiplicity uncertainty. Supplementary tree comparisons have their own support and budgets. We do not compare their marginal intervals to establish a paired difference. The inference timings in the generated table exclude diagnostic acquisition and transfer, and cannot certify end-to-end operation. The TCN findings sharpen the conditions of the second and third questions of this paper rather than supplying a new general learning algorithm.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{Reading.} At the pedestal top a nowcast built from the CES history and two fast
diagnostics has a lower MSE than monotone interpolation that waits for the next CES
value, and under two of the four designs a lower MSE than a two-sided GP; in time order
no difference from the two-sided GP is established, and under the random design its
median absolute error is the higher one (Table~\ref{tab:vsref}). Without the fast
diagnostics the MSE of the nowcast is higher than that of the two-sided GP under all four
designs. The gain is in the frames in which the value moves. On both devices an
electron channel carries part of it: on KSTAR the ECEI half alone has an increment under
three of the four designs with the history \AgeMedianS\,s old, and on MAST the increment
comes with the Thomson \Te{}. A reason to look at an electron channel is that electrons
and ions exchange energy within ten to a few tens of milliseconds in deuterium at a
density of a few $10^{19}$\,m$^{-3}$ and an electron temperature of several hundred
eV~\citep{Wesson2011Tokamaks}. What this paper tests is the estimate, not that reasoning:
on KSTAR no input is an electron temperature (the ECEI level at the edge depends on the
density as well and is not calibrated in the files), no equilibrium places the chord, and
the increment is not shown to be specific to its radius.

\emph{Value for monitoring and control.} The pedestal-top ion temperature is a quantity
that control asks for: a published real-time controller of edge-localized modes on KSTAR
runs a preprogrammed waveform for lack of a direct pedestal
diagnostic~\citep{Shin2022PreemptiveDriven}. What is shown here is an estimate that
fits into a 10\,ms frame and that, with the previous frame visible, lies within 10 per
cent of the level in two frames out of three. That is short of the 10 per cent of the
requirement documents, and nothing was run on a plant control system: use in control is a
prospect. The channels of the files sit at the edge, so nothing is said about the core;
the method itself needs, for any chord, its CES history and a fast diagnostic at its
radius. On MAST, where both exist at every radius, it was run from the core to the edge
(\S\ref{sec:res:mast}).

\emph{Relation to readers of the spectrum.} A possible modular system would use NN-CES~\citep{Lee2026NeuralNetworkbased} to analyse each acquired spectrum and update the CES history when its result becomes available. Between updates, the temporal estimator would combine that history with the available BES and ECEI to maintain a nowcast. Faster spectral analysis could refresh the anchors sooner, while also shortening the interval over which a nowcast is needed. The usefulness of the combined system would therefore have to be measured with its actual report timing and outputs. Here the history and targets come from conventional analysis: NN-CES outputs were not used to fit or evaluate the estimator, and no coupled pipeline was tested. The two methods address complementary estimation stages; their relative accuracy is not established by this study.

\emph{What use would still need.} Fast samples delivered in real time; the time of the
CES analysis itself; a model that follows changing conditions without a refit, for which
a recurrent network trained across delayed and thinned CES histories is the candidate;
and a reference that does not come from the same analysis as the history.

\emph{Limits.}
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \emph{Target.} Both targets are built from the reported C\,VI temperature: there is
no per-frame fit error, a bias of the analysis lasting longer than 50\,ms is in the
target, and equality with the main-ion temperature is not tested.
\item \emph{Rows.} Rows without a visible CES value are not scored, and the references
that wait cannot be formed on the last measured row of a block (\SuppTab{tab:sets}).
\item \emph{Intervals.} They resample clusters of the scored rows and do not include the
variability of the fit; the chronological design scores 14 to 19 clusters, so lower ends
close to zero are to be read with caution. The registered statements are many and no
correction for multiplicity is applied; each is judged by its own interval.
\item \emph{Selection.} All data had been read before the tests were registered; the
estimator's inputs were fixed in an earlier batch on the same discharges; no untouched
hold-out exists.
\item \emph{Scope.} One chord, one campaign and one device for the sizes; MAST carries
the direction only.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

For the charge-exchange ion temperature on the pedestal-top chord of KSTAR, where the
measurement is weak, the causal estimator with the lowest error found here, with every
value up to the previous frame at hand, is a Gaussian
process on the CES history followed by a boosted regression on two dozen scalars of that
history and of BES and ECEI at the location. Without waiting for the next value it has a
lower mean squared error than monotone cubic interpolation, which waits, under random,
cluster-blocked, rolling-origin and chronological validation, and than a two-sided
Gaussian process under the first two; in time order no difference from that process is
established. The
fast diagnostics carry the difference to the two-sided process, their gain is in the
larger errors, and the estimate takes about two milliseconds. In use, where the CES value
is late or sparse, the fast diagnostics matter more, on KSTAR and, with Thomson
scattering, on MAST; but the estimator has to be refitted for the condition of use, it
does not replace a reading of the frame once that exists, its gain is not shown to be
specific to the radius, and under the beam-modulation schedule it is not established for
the last block of shots. The measurements that would extend or bound these statements are
the field and ECEI settings of each shot, per-frame fit uncertainties and spectra, an
equilibrium for the chord, the output of a real-time reader on the same discharges, a
model with state trained across the conditions of use, and discharges that have not been
read.
